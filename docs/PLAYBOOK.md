# Playbook

How hackathon applications, travel money, sponsor prizes and judging tend to work. This is community knowledge plus what organisers publish about their own process. Where a claim comes from a specific event's site, it says so; the rest is general practice and will not hold everywhere.

## Getting in

**Apply the week applications open.** Most large events review on a rolling or priority basis. Published examples from the 2026–27 cycle:

| Event | Event date | Deadline | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| HackMIT | Sep 19–20 | ~Jul 4 | 11 weeks |
| MHacks | Oct 3–4 | Early Aug 7 · regular Sep 12 | 3–8 weeks |
| Cal Hacks | October | Priority Sep 13 · regular Sep 20 | ~4 weeks |
| HackPrinceton | Nov 13–15 | Sep 28 | 7 weeks |
| HackUTD | Nov 7–8 | Priority Oct 3 | 5 weeks |
| PennApps | Feb 5–7 | Priority Dec 6 · regular Jan 6 | 4–9 weeks |

**Know the odds.** TreeHacks reportedly drew about 15,000 applications for roughly 1,000 places in 2026, and HackUTD reports 3,500+ applicants for 1,200+ places. HackMIT guarantees places for MIT students, so outside places are scarcer than the headline number suggests. Home-school students usually get priority everywhere.

**Write the essay about something you built.** Organisers who have written about admissions say they read for evidence that you make things without being told to. A specific side project with a link beats a list of courses. Link a GitHub or Devpost profile that has something in it.

**Apply as an individual, then form a team.** Most applications are reviewed per person. Being admitted alone is normal, and every event runs team formation.

**Beginner-friendly does not mean easy to get into, and selective does not mean experts only.** The big events deliberately admit first-timers. Say so if you are one.

**Waitlists move.** A meaningful share of admitted hackers do not confirm. Answer RSVP emails the day they arrive, and if you are waitlisted, stay reachable through the week of the event.

## Travel money

- Check the box. Reimbursement usually has to be requested on the application itself (HackUTD) or appears in your portal after admission (PennApps).
- Priority applicants are funded first. HackUTD states this directly.
- Caps are regional. TreeHacks and Cal Hacks both describe flight coverage with regional caps, so a cross-country flight may be only partly covered.
- Some events pay nothing. BoilerMake says it cannot offer travel reimbursement; HackHarvard provides no lodging.
- Ask your own university. ACM chapters, engineering schools and dean's offices often have small travel grants for exactly this.
- Keep every receipt and submit within the stated window. Late claims are the most common reason people are not repaid.

## Prizes and sponsor tracks

**Sponsor tracks are less crowded than the main prize.** Everyone enters "Best Overall". A sponsor track might get a dozen serious entries.

**The same tracks recur.** In fall 2026, Capital One's "Best Use of Nessie" ($300 per team member) and Photon's messaging-agent track (cash, credits and an interview fast-track) appeared at both HackUTD and HackHarvard. MLH partner prizes repeat at nearly every MLH member event. Learn an API once and you can reuse the head start.

**Read the track list before the event.** Challenge descriptions are often published days ahead. HackUTD's 2026 tracks ranged from $2,048 per team to RTX 5080 GPUs.

**Talk to the sponsor engineers on Saturday afternoon.** They judge their own track. Ask what they would be impressed by. Teams that do this build what the judge wants; teams that do not are guessing.

**Sponsor booths are recruiting.** Several events advertise early sponsor interviews. Bring a résumé link.

## Building

- Decide the demo first, then build only what the demo needs.
- One working flow beats five half-working features. Judges see each project for two to four minutes.
- Have a recorded backup of the demo. Venue wifi fails at the worst moment.
- Deploy early. A live URL the judge can open on their phone is persuasive.
- Stop adding features about three hours before the deadline and spend that time on the pitch and the Devpost page. Many events do a first-round cut from Devpost alone.
- Sleep at least a few hours at a 36-hour event. Tired teams break things on Sunday morning.

## Judging

Rubrics vary, but most score some mix of technical difficulty, originality, usefulness and presentation. In practice:

- Lead with the problem and a ten-second demo, then explain how it works.
- Say plainly what is real and what is mocked. Judges ask, and being caught is worse than admitting it.
- At expo-style judging you will pitch ten or more times. Rotate who speaks.

## Company events are a different game

- **JPMorganChase Code for Good** is a two-day build for a nonprofit that feeds the Software Engineer Program internship. The application is a résumé followed by a HackerRank challenge, and the 2026 US deadline was in early October.
- **Citadel datathons** include a timed assessment before selection and are openly a recruiting channel.
- **AI-lab hackathons** (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and others, often hosted with Cerebral Valley) are curated by profile and past projects, are announced only a few weeks ahead, and are not listed on MLH. Follow [cerebralvalley.ai/events](https://cerebralvalley.ai/events) and Luma.
- **Imagine Cup** now judges startups more than weekend projects. Enter with something you have been building for months.
- **Swift Student Challenge** has a three-week window in February. Winners generally built their playground before it opened.

## Things that no longer exist

Old lists still mention these, so you can stop looking: Google Code Jam, Kick Start and Hash Code (ended 2023), Optiver Ready Trader Go (no edition since 2023), and the Interactive Brokers Collegiate Trading Olympiad (discontinued).
